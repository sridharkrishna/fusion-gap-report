The Fusion Gap Report

Nuclear fusion is how the sun and the stars produce energy. It could be a near-limitless, clean source of power and holds great promise for the future. Unlike nuclear fission, which releases energy by splitting the nuclei of heavy atoms such as uranium into smaller ones, fusion combines the nuclei of two isotopes of hydrogen to form helium, releasing huge amounts of energy.

Scientists have understood for about a century that fusion powers the stars, and have tried to harness it for electricity since the 1950s. Only now do many of the hurdles to commercialising it look surmountable. Recent experiments have produced more fusion energy than was delivered to the fuel, and have held hot plasma for longer periods, giving new hope for commercialisation.

Until recently, fusion research was largely confined to government laboratories and international institutions set up by groups of countries. Private companies have since raised billions, and the enormous energy needs of artificial intelligence have drawn in large technology firms: Microsoft has agreed to buy power from Helion, and Google has invested in Commonwealth Fusion Systems and TAE Technologies.

I am tracking the progress in nuclear fusion and identifying the gaps to commercial production of electricity from fusion reactors. These monthly dashboards reflect the progress and the gaps.

How the scores move. A score changes only on a new physical demonstration or an institutional decision with legal effect. Announcements, simulations, design papers and single funding rounds don't move scores. Each issue explains every score, lists its sources, and records corrections.

Scores are analyst judgement grounded in cited milestones, not an official index. Each issue works best on a laptop; on a phone, use the table view.
